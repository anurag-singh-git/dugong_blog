## New post

**Nº 142 - "How to spot serial returners on Shopify before you refund them again"**
`how-to-spot-serial-returners-on-shopify.html` - Priya Singh - 2026-11-17 - 2,525 words -
9 min read

Pain point: a refund request arrives and the order page shows one return. It does not show the
customer's other six. Shopify's fraud tooling runs before the sale and stops there, so the entire
reverse side of the order, the part where money actually leaves, has no native surface: no per
customer return rate, no return behavior filter in customer segments, no report between a single
return and a store level total.

Why this topic: high demand, heavy public complaining, and almost nothing written about the
mechanism. What exists is vendor material about return fraud in the abstract. Nobody has put
together that Shopify's own returns metric counts pre-shipment size swaps, so any per customer rate
built on it is wrong in the direction that punishes the stores with generous exchange policies;
that merchants have been rebuilding the same calculation by hand in Flow's Run Code step since
early 2024 and have converged on a specification without anyone writing it down; or that the
counting was never the hard part, because the signal that separates a sizing problem from a person
is whether the reason text tells the same story each time.

Facts used, all checked live this run:
- Shopify Community, "Returns metric in Analytics is misleading, should reflect actual returns,
  not all refund events" (June 2026): an apparel founder reports the Analytics Returns metric
  counts every refund event including pre-shipment order edits for size adjustments, giving a
  reported rate of about 25 percent against an actual of about 3 percent, and one seven day stretch
  showing minus 572 dollars with zero physical returns. He calls Shopify's March 2026 split into
  "sales reversal" and "quantity returned" a rename, noting the Returns over time report still used
  the old field with a deprecation warning to replace `returns` with `sales_reversals`, and that
  the new figure still bundles edits, swaps, cancellations and physical returns. A second member
  calls it "a legitimate analytics gap" and says edits and returns are "fundamentally different
  events". No Shopify staff reply, no resolution.
- Shopify Community, "How to detect a potential return abuser in customer orders?" (opened March
  2024, last reply October 2026): the merchant wanted a Flow notification on customers with 3+
  orders and net sales under 150 dollars, and reports Shopify Support telling them Flow cannot
  check all order history. The answering reply gives two routes, `order.customer.numberOfOrders`
  and `amountSpent.amount` which both include the triggering order, or Get order data in advanced
  mode capped at 100 orders with a query on `customer_id` plus
  `return_status:"return_requested,in_progress,returned"`. A follow-up raises that counting
  returned orders is not counting returned items on a partial return, which needs Run Code summing
  `returnLineItems` quantities. An October 2026 reply proposes alerting only when refunds exceed
  about 40 percent of spend across three or more orders, so a size exchange does not trigger it.
- Shopify Community, "Shopify Flow to calculate return abusers" (November 2024): a Plus merchant
  builds units returned over the last 100 orders, hits a nested loop bug in Run Code that another
  member fixes, then abandons units for refund dollars over gross sales. Their own framing is that
  about 20 percent of some products are faulty so refunds are routine, while separately some
  customers return 60 to 70 percent of what they buy.
- Shopify Community, "Tips for identifying serial return abuse on your store" (April 2026): export
  six months of returns, sort by customer email, look at anyone above three returns, treat a return
  to order ratio above 50 percent as worth investigating; returns filed one to three days after
  delivery with vague reasons as the wardrobing signal; returns filed Monday or Tuesday against
  orders delivered Thursday or Friday as event wardrobing; a product at roughly three times the
  typical rate as a listing problem rather than fraud; and the chargeback cross reference, where a
  customer refunded through a return who also won a chargeback "was refunded twice". The author's
  own caveat, carried into the post: "None of this is foolproof."
- Fraud Filter was retired on 31 January 2025 with Shopify pointing merchants at Flow; the built in
  risk indicator scores low, medium or high for stolen card risk at the moment of the sale; Shopify
  Protect covers chargebacks on Shop Pay. All three are pre-purchase or chargeback scoped, which is
  the structural point the post rests on.
- NRF and Happy Returns, 2025 Retail Returns Landscape (15 October 2025): 849.9 billion dollars of
  returns in 2025, 15.8 percent of annual sales, an estimated 19.3 percent of online sales, and
  9 percent of all returns fraudulent.
- Shopify Help Center, Get order data and Conditions in Shopify Flow, for the 100 order cap and the
  nested field conditions that make the detection half of this real.

Playbook shipped: score the customer rather than the order on value returned over value bought
across the whole history with a floor on order count; separate exchanges, order edits and
cancellations from physical returns before dividing; read the delivery to request interval, the
weekday pair and whether the reason text tells the same story each time; cross check refunds
against chargebacks for the same window; change the offer rather than the welcome, with manual
capture, store credit and withdrawn free return shipping instead of a ban; tell fulfillment before
the pick so the pack is photographed and serialised while the box is still open; and report the top
of the list weekly beside the products returning at three times their category. Honest limits
stated in the post: it cannot prove intent, it cannot stop a determined abuser opening a new
account, and it will occasionally put an unlucky customer near the top, which is why the response
at the top of the list is a review and not a ban.

Overlap handled deliberately: Nº 4 (returns and exchanges) owns the approval and exchange
mechanics, Nº 52 (returns the customer never sends back) owns the quiet return, Nº 14 (chargebacks)
owns the dispute, and Nº 89 (blocking a customer) owns why a ban does not hold. This post links to
all four in prose and keeps its weight on the per customer number, the analytics field that
poisons it, and the two and a half year community record of merchants building it by hand.
