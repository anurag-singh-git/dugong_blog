How to send payment reminders for unpaid draft orders on Shopify

You build the draft order, click Send invoice, and Shopify sends one email, once. No reminder if it sits unpaid, no expiry, no hold on the stock it promises. Here is why unpaid drafts pile up, and the playbook that chases every invoice until it pays or dies.

PS Priya Singh · Commerce Research, Dugong

A boutique stockist in Savannah emails your letterpress studio on Monday morning: sixty of the botanical greeting cards, two dozen of the linen notebooks, can you invoice me? You build the draft order in four minutes. Wholesale prices, her address, a shipping line, $1,140 all in. You click Send invoice, Shopify confirms it went, and you get back to the press with the small satisfaction of a sale closed before lunch.

On Friday the draft still says Invoice sent. Not because she changed her mind. Her yes is sitting right there in your inbox. Your invoice is also sitting in hers, under forty newer emails, in the folder of things she meant to do on Monday. Shopify has not reminded her. It has not told you. And here is the part merchants keep rediscovering the hard way: it never will.

The Send invoice button sends one email with a checkout link. That is the complete feature. The draft's status flips from Open to Invoice sent and then nothing else happens, ever, on any timeline. There is no follow-up to the customer, no alert to you on day five, no view that sorts drafts by waiting longest, no total of what is outstanding. The Drafts tab shows your $1,140 wholesale order with exactly the same quiet indifference as a test draft from March.

One email is the entire feature

Draft orders are where Shopify's tidy self-serve story meets the messy half of commerce: phone orders, wholesale accounts, custom work, repairs, the customer who cannot check out because her company card blocks online payments. Every one of those sales ends the same way, with an invoice email and a wait. And everyone who has ever sent invoices knows the shape of the wait: some people pay within the hour, and the rest fully intended to. The difference between the two groups is rarely willingness. It is whether anyone followed up.

Shopify does not follow up, and it makes following up by hand harder than it looks. The platform does not even record which email address an invoice actually went to. You can type any address into the To field when you send, and the draft keeps no note of it, so any tool that wants to chase the invoice later has to assume the customer on file was the recipient. The automation platform Mechanic ships a follow-up task for exactly this job, and its own documentation has to admit that limitation before it does anything else.

The reminder machinery exists, one door over

The strange part is that Shopify has a perfectly good reminder system. Assign payment terms to an order, Net 15 or Net 30 or due on a date, and Settings, Notifications, Payment reminders will send up to five automatic emails per order, timed anywhere from the due date to thirty days after it, while the order wears an Overdue badge that actually means something.

The catch is in the noun. Payment reminders apply to orders, and a draft with an invoice out is not an order. It only becomes one when the customer pays or you mark it paid. So the documents most in need of automatic reminders, the invoices you sent to people who have not paid yet, live in the one tab the reminder system cannot see. Merchants keep landing on the forums asking for exactly this, and the thread titled Workflow for payment reminders without due dates ends where these threads usually end, at a list of apps.

There is a workaround with teeth, if you know the trick: complete the draft as payment due later with terms attached. Now it is a real order and the reminder machinery wakes up. It is also a real order number in your reports, counted as a sale you have not been paid for, holding committed stock, and if the customer never pays, you get to discover that unpaid orders do not cancel themselves either. You have not solved the chase. You have moved it into a more expensive room.

The stock your invoice quietly promised

While the invoice waits, the sixty cards it promises are still for sale. Sending an invoice reserves nothing. Your storefront, your other channels, and every other draft can sell those units all week, because nothing in Shopify treats invoiced stock as spoken for. The stockist pays on Thursday for cards that sold out on Tuesday, and now you are holding her money and an apology: refund the line, or open a backorder conversation she never signed up for.

Shopify's answer is the Reserve items button, and it is an honest little feature with three quiet flaws: it is manual, per draft, every time; it expires on a date you pick and releases silently when that date passes; and nothing connects it to whether the invoice was paid. Wholesale merchants were still filing this gap as unacceptable on the community forums in May of this year, the thread's author pointing out that an invoice neither deducts nor reliably reserves inventory, which leaves the store exposed for exactly as long as a B2B buyer takes to pay. The older thread asking What is the point of Reserve Items on a Draft order? has been collecting confused replies for years.

◆ NOTE Drafts also never expire. The checkout link in an invoice works for as long as the draft exists, at the prices the draft was built with. Raise your wholesale prices in January and the forgotten quote from September is still payable in February, at September prices, for stock you may no longer carry. The Drafts tab is a pile of open offers with no expiry dates, and Shopify will not mention one was accepted until the order appears.

Why Flow cannot chase an invoice

The reflex is to build the reminder in Shopify Flow, and Flow gets you exactly one step in. Its only draft trigger is Draft order created, which fires the moment the draft is made, before you have even sent the invoice, and never speaks of the draft again. There is no trigger for invoice sent. There is no event for still unpaid after three days. Flow does ship a Send payment reminder action, but it hangs off payment schedules, which means payment terms, which means orders again.

Even the email is the wrong shape. Flow's messages are built for notifying your own team, and merchants who try to bend it into customer-facing follow-up, in threads like Shopify Flow Triggered by Open Draft Orders, run into the same walls: no clean way to write to the customer with the draft's details filled in, no way to schedule the second nudge, and a polite suggestion to try an app. The apps exist and they are fine at what they do, which is a fixed cadence and a template: twenty-four hours after the invoice, then daily, same message for everyone. Better than silence. But a metronome cannot tell your oldest stockist from a stranger, cannot read a reply that says can we make it forty instead, and cannot see that the draft it keeps politely nudging is promising stock that ran out yesterday.

What the automation actually has to do

The job is not send more emails. The job is the one a good bookkeeper with taste would do: know what is out, chase it with judgment, notice what each invoice is holding, and kill the dead ones before they cause accidents. As a Dugong playbook, in plain prose: 

# trigger
On invoice sent, on customer reply, on the clock

# steps
1. Log every invoice as it leaves: who, what,
   how much, and which stock it promises
2. Remind on a cadence that reads the room:
   day 2 gentle, day 5 direct, day 10 last
   call, stopping on a reply or a payment
3. Read the answers and route them: questions
   to you, edits into the draft, "paid you
   Tuesday" checked against the money
4. Watch the stock each draft promises and
   flag it before the payment outlives it
5. Escalate by size and history: the $1,900
   wholesale draft gets you, not a template
6. Expire the dead ones: last call, cancel,
   release the stock, keep the tab honest
7. Count weekly: sent, paid, days to pay,
   and who never pays without three nudges

Step two is where the fixed-cadence tools crack. The right day-5 email to a first-time buyer of a custom piece is a link and a deadline. The right day-5 email to your oldest stockist, three years of on-time payments and one slow month, is warmth, her buyer's name spelled right, and an offer to split the order into two invoices. Same day, same status, entirely different letter. Choosing between them is not a rule you can write in advance. It is a reading of the whole account, the same species of judgment as deciding what a high risk flag actually means or which wholesale application deserves a same-day yes.

Step three is the half nobody budgets for. Reminders generate replies, and the replies land in your inbox, not in the draft. Can you make it forty instead of sixty is a draft edit and a fresh invoice, sent the same hour. I paid this by transfer on Tuesday is a check against the money that actually arrived, then a paid mark or a gentle correction. What is this invoice? is an escalation to you, today, because either the stockist forgot or someone is being invoiced who should not be. An automation that sends but does not read is just a louder metronome.

Steps four and six are inventory policy wearing an accounts hat. If the cards the draft promises drop below the invoiced quantity before payment lands, you want the flag while choices still exist: reserve what is left, warn the stockist, or pull stock from the next production run. And expiry is a decision you write once: quotes die at thirty days with a last call email, wholesale gets forty-five and a phone call first, bespoke work stays open while the conversation is alive. The automation enforces the policy, cancels the corpse, releases the stock, and the Drafts tab finally tells the truth about your pipeline.

Why this is a compiler problem, not an app problem

Your actual follow-up policy is a paragraph. Remind at two, five, and ten days, but never nudge the Marion account without showing me first. Anything over $1,500 unpaid for a week is a phone call, not an email. If a draft is holding the last units of anything, tell me on day three. Kill retail quotes at thirty days, keep bespoke open, and anyone who needed three nudges twice in a row pays a deposit next time. That paragraph is the whole system, and it does not fit in an app's settings page, because it is shaped like your customers and your margins. A compiler that turns the paragraph into the running workflow is the point, and it is the same argument behind every automation merchants never build: the valuable workflow is shaped like your store, not like a feature list.

Run the numbers on the letterpress studio. Last month it sent 30 draft invoices. Eighteen paid without a word, because some people are like that. Twelve needed chasing: seven paid after the day-2 note, three after the day-5, one after the last call, and one died at thirty days, canceled with its stock released the same minute. Median days to pay fell from eight to three once the cadence went live, not because anyone became a better customer, but because the invoice stopped being the thing everyone forgot. The owner's part in all of it was one Friday summary and a single decision about a slow-paying account. The Drafts tab holds nothing older than the policy allows, which is a sentence no Shopify merchant has ever said out loud.

The workflow worth building this week

Start with the number Shopify will not show you. Open Drafts, filter by Invoice sent, and add up what is sitting there. Most stores that send invoices find a month of quiet revenue in that tab, unchased not because anyone decided to let it go, but because no system ever put it in front of them. That total is the business case, and it took ninety seconds.

Then run the trust curve you would run with any new hire. For the first two weeks, let the workflow log every invoice, draft every reminder, and show you each one before it goes. You are checking its reading of your customers against your own, the same way you would check the paperwork it sends after the sale. When it has been right fifty times, let the routine cadence run end to end and keep only the escalations: the big drafts, the odd replies, the accounts you have history with.

Describe it the way you would brief that hire on their first morning. When an invoice goes out, put it on the list. If nobody pays in two days, remind them nicely. Five days, remind them plainly. Ten days, tell them it is the last call, and mean it. Stop the moment they answer, and bring me anything strange. If the stock an invoice promises starts running out, tell me before it does, not after. Kill anything past thirty days and put the stock back. And on Fridays, tell me what went out, what came in, and who should be paying deposits from now on. That is the whole brief. The compiler handles the events, the timers, and the ledger, and the stockist in Savannah pays on Wednesday, because on Wednesday morning the invoice was the top email in her inbox instead of the fortieth.

◆ COMMON QUESTIONS

Does Shopify automatically send payment reminders for unpaid draft order invoices?

No. The Send invoice button sends one email with a checkout link, the draft's status flips to Invoice sent, and nothing follows: no reminder to the customer, no alert to you, no expiry on the draft. Shopify does have automatic payment reminders, up to five per order, timed to the due date or up to thirty days after, but they only apply to orders with payment terms assigned, and a draft order does not become an order until someone pays it or you mark it paid. You can work around this by completing the draft as payment due later with terms attached, which creates a real unpaid order that the reminder system can see, at the cost of an order number that pollutes your reports and has to be canceled by hand if it never pays. Otherwise, follow-up is yours to build with apps or automation.

Do draft orders reserve inventory when you send an invoice?

No. Sending an invoice changes nothing about inventory: every unit on the draft stays available on your storefront and on every channel while the customer decides. If the items sell out before the invoice is paid, the checkout link still works, and you end up holding money for stock you no longer have. Shopify's Reserve items feature is a manual step on each draft with an expiry date you pick, it releases silently when that date passes, and wholesale merchants were still describing the gap as unacceptable on the community forums in May 2026, because the exposure lasts exactly as long as a B2B buyer takes to pay. The reliable pattern is to treat invoiced stock as spoken for: track what each open draft promises, flag drafts when their stock runs low, and reserve or release deliberately instead of by accident.

How long do Shopify draft orders stay open, and should I cancel old ones?

Forever. Draft orders have no expiry: an unpaid draft sits in the Drafts tab until someone pays it or you delete it, and the invoice's checkout link keeps working the whole time at the prices the draft was built with. That is how a quote from last autumn gets paid in the spring at autumn prices, for items that may have been discontinued or repriced since. The fix is a written expiry policy the automation enforces: a last call email at a deadline you choose, then cancel the draft, release any reserved stock, and note why. Thirty days suits most retail quotes, wholesale terms often run longer, and bespoke work can stay open as long as the conversation is alive. What matters is that expiry is a decision, not an accident of nobody remembering the draft exists.
