How to automatically follow up on unpaid draft order invoices on Shopify

The customer said yes. You built the draft order, sent the invoice, and then nothing. Shopify will never remind them, and the follow-up everyone assumes exists does not. Here is why, and the playbook that gets invoices paid without you chasing a single one.

PS Priya Singh · Commerce Research, Dugong

A furniture maker in Asheville, North Carolina finishes pricing a walnut dining table on a Tuesday morning. The customer has already said yes twice, once by email and once by Instagram DM, so she builds the draft order, checks the numbers, and clicks send invoice. Shopify emails him a tidy checkout link. He replies eleven minutes later: looks great, paying tonight.

Tonight comes and goes. So does the weekend. On Friday she opens the Drafts screen and finds the table sitting in a list with fourteen other open drafts: a phone order from a regular, two school purchase orders, a batch of custom cutting boards for a wedding. All of them agreed. None of them paid. Her question is the reasonable one: when does Shopify remind him? The answer is worth pinning above every merchant's desk. It does not. The invoice email goes out once, the moment you click the button, and that is the last time Shopify will ever mention it to anyone.

Here is the asymmetry that makes the silence strange. If that same customer had wandered into her online store and abandoned a cart, a recovery sequence would already be scheduled. A stranger who never spoke to her gets three automated emails. The customer who negotiated, agreed, and asked to be invoiced gets zero. The closer the sale, the less the platform helps you finish it.

Why Shopify never reminds anyone

Merchants keep rediscovering this gap one layer at a time, and each layer looks like it should be the answer. The first is abandoned checkout recovery, which does not apply: a draft order is not a checkout, so an unpaid invoice never joins the abandoned checkout list and never triggers a recovery email. The community thread asking How can I add unpaid draft orders to the abandoned checkout list? has a one-word answer wearing a paragraph as a disguise, and its sibling, Enable Abandoned Carts for Draft Orders, was still collecting replies at the end of 2025.

The second layer is the payment reminders setting, which exists and is narrower than its name. It only works on orders that carry payment terms, meaning a due date, and it only fires on or after that date, with one fixed template for everyone. An ordinary invoice with no terms attached reminds no one, ever, which merchants tend to discover after a test email that never arrives. A school that pays by purchase order on net 30 cannot be nudged seven days before the deadline, because reminding early is not a concept the feature has. And its cousin, the bank transfer order that sits pending forever, has its own version of this problem at the other end of the pipeline.

The third layer is Shopify Flow, and this one stings, because Flow is supposed to be the automation escape hatch. Flow can watch draft orders, wait, check conditions. What it cannot do is email your customer: Flow sends email to staff, a limitation Shopify staff confirmed on the forums in 2022 with a promise to look into it, and the thread asking was still active in late 2025. The one action called send payment reminder is reserved for B2B payment schedules on Shopify Plus, and it emails the company's main contact only. Four features, each of which almost does it. None of which does.

The Drafts screen is not a to-do list. It is revenue that already said yes, waiting on a follow-up Shopify will never send.

The link that outlives the price

The silence has a strange twin. While Shopify does nothing to chase an open invoice, it also does nothing to close one. The checkout link in that invoice email keeps working for as long as the draft stays open, which in practice means indefinitely. One merchant put it plainly on the forums: the invoice link never expires, and a customer can come back after a year and buy at the old price. For any store whose costs move, and in the last few years that is every store, a stale quote is not a dead lead. It is a discount coupon you do not remember issuing, waiting in someone's inbox.

And there is no clean way to end one. Draft orders have no canceled status, a feature request that has been sitting open since 2025. Your native options are two: delete the draft, which erases the record of what was quoted and to whom, or leave the link alive forever. Shopify's own housekeeping is blunter still, quietly deleting recent draft orders after a year of inactivity, paper trail and all. Merchants bridge the gap with Flow recipes that delete drafts on a timer, which fixes the stale price by burning the sales history along with it.

◆ NOTE The trap is symmetric. Chase too little and agreed revenue evaporates. Leave the links alive and old prices become redeemable. A quote needs a lifecycle: sent, reminded, decided, expired. Shopify gives it two states, open and deleted, and nothing in between.

Why chasing by hand fails quietly

Most stores respond to all this the artisanal way: someone is supposed to remember. It fails for reasons that have nothing to do with discipline. Invoice follow-up is nobody's job, so it lives in the dead zone between sales and admin, done in batches whenever guilt accumulates. The Drafts screen gives it no help, because there is no aging view, no sent six days ago and opened twice column, just a list sorted by date that makes you do the arithmetic yourself. On a busy week, an open draft from the 9th and an open draft from this morning look identical.

Then there is the awkwardness, which is the real killer. Chasing money makes merchants feel like debt collectors, so each reminder gets written from scratch, tuned somewhere between apologetic and passive-aggressive, and often skipped entirely in favor of hoping. Meanwhile the value of the follow-up decays by the day. At day two a reminder is a formality between people who just spoke. At day thirty it is a cold call about a purchase the customer barely remembers. And none of this appears in any report: no metric counts agreed and evaporated, so the leak stays invisible while everyone works hard.

What the automation actually has to do

The fix is not one reminder email. It is the collections discipline of a good bookkeeper, applied to every draft the moment it goes out, without the founder writing anything. As a Dugong playbook, in plain prose: 

# trigger
On invoice sent, on customer reply, and daily
   over the open drafts list

# steps
1. Read every open draft in context: age,
   amount, customer history, channel, and
   what was actually agreed
2. Sequence the follow-up: a friendly nudge
   at day 2, a question at day 7, a
   decision at day 14
3. Match the tone to the buyer: first-timer,
   regular, or accounts payable on net terms
4. Escalate over the threshold: big invoices
   get a drafted note for a human to send
5. Expire the quote on schedule: close the
   draft, kill the link, offer a fresh
   price on request
6. Reconcile on payment: tag, thank, hand to
   fulfillment, note which touch worked
7. Report the pipeline weekly: open drafts
   by age and value, and the recovery rate

Steps one through three are reading, not scheduling, and that is the difference between follow-up and spam. A timer can send the same template to everyone on day two. It cannot notice that this draft is a school district that always pays, just slowly, and should be reminded before its due date at the accounts payable address, not after it at the teacher's. Or that this one is a regular who pays from her phone every Friday and needs nothing but patience. Or that the silence on a custom piece usually means a change request, which is why the day seven email asks does anything need changing rather than did you pay. The same judgment that reads a wholesale application before approving it is what reads an unpaid invoice before chasing it.

Step five is the one merchants forget is possible. A quote should die on a schedule you chose, not live forever in an inbox. The automation closes the draft at day 21, archives what was agreed into the customer record first, and sends the courteous exit: prices change, this quote has expired, a fresh one is a reply away. That kills the zombie link problem at the root, and it gives the whole sequence a spine. A deadline with a warm exit reads as professionalism. An open loop reads as a store that forgot.

And step six closes the loop the moment money lands: tag the order, thank the customer, hand it to fulfillment, and write down which touch did the work, because that number is how next month's sequence gets better.

Why this is a compiler problem, not an app problem

There are payment reminder apps, and if all you need is a timetable they are fine. What they hold is a schedule. Your collections policy is a paragraph: nudge everyone at day two, kindly. At day seven, ask if anything needs changing, because half our silences are change requests. Schools get reminded five days before the due date, at the accounts payable address, never on a Friday afternoon. Anything over five hundred dollars gets a note written for me to send myself. Quotes die at 21 days, except commissions, which get 45. Regulars never get more than one reminder, ever. That paragraph is yours. It changes with your margins, your customers, and your nerve, and no settings page can hold it. A compiler that turns the paragraph into the running workflow can, which is the same argument behind the automations no one builds: the valuable workflow is shaped like your store, not like a feature list.

Run the Asheville shop through it. She sends about 38 invoices a month, and 21 pay on their own within two days. The other 17 used to be memory work. With the playbook running, nine clear at the day two nudge, which is nothing more than the link resurfaced with a friendly line. Four more clear at the day seven question, one of them after the reply revealed an order change the automation surfaced for her to approve. The $1,900 commission crossed her five hundred dollar threshold, so the note was drafted and she sent it herself, paid that evening. The two school purchase orders rode their own track, reminded at accounts payable before the due date. The last one never answered, and at day 21 it closed with the quote expired note, link dead, record archived. Her total follow-up writing for the month was one email, and the weekly summary told her something the Drafts screen never could: average open draft age fell from nineteen days to four.

The workflow worth building this week

Start with the number you do not have. Open the Drafts screen, filter to open, and add it up. Invoice-heavy stores routinely find weeks of revenue parked there, and the useful cut is age: anything older than fourteen days is not pipeline, it is evaporation in progress. That total is what the follow-up sequence is worth, and it is usually the most persuasive spreadsheet cell a merchant sees all year.

Then run it supervised before you run it loose. For the first two weeks, let the workflow read the drafts, pick the timing, and write every reminder, but click send yourself. You are checking its judgment against yours, tone included, the same trust curve you would run with the invoicing itself. Once it has matched your instincts fifty times, let the routine cases run end to end and keep the personal notes and the odd ducks for yourself.

Describe it the way you would brief a new hire on their first morning. When we send an invoice, watch it. If nothing happens in two days, nudge, kindly, just the link again. At a week, ask if something needs changing. Schools get reminded before their due date at the right inbox. Anything big, write it and show me first. Kill quotes at three weeks so old prices cannot come back to bite us, and be gracious about it. And tell me on Fridays what is open, what is aging, and what worked. That is the brief. The compiler handles the fields, the timers, and the sequences, and the walnut table gets paid for on Wednesday instead of becoming a story the maker tells other merchants as a warning.

◆ COMMON QUESTIONS

Does Shopify automatically remind customers about unpaid draft order invoices?

No. When you click send invoice on a draft order, Shopify emails the checkout link once, and that is the only email it will ever send about that invoice. Unpaid draft orders never appear in the abandoned checkout list, so recovery emails do not fire for them, a gap merchants have asked about on the community forums for years. The payment reminders setting sounds like the answer but is narrower than its name: it only works on orders that carry payment terms with a due date, and it only sends on or after that date, with one fixed template. Shopify Flow cannot close the gap either, because Flow sends email to your staff, not to your customers, and its send payment reminder action is tied to B2B payment schedules on Shopify Plus. Following up on an ordinary unpaid invoice is entirely your job, which is exactly why it belongs to automation.

Do Shopify draft order invoice links expire?

No, and merchants discover this the expensive way. The checkout link in a draft order invoice keeps working for as long as the draft stays open, so a customer can come back months or a year later and pay the price you quoted then, not the price you charge now. There is no canceled status for a draft order, a feature request still sitting open on the community forums, so your two native options are deleting the draft, which erases the record, or leaving the link alive indefinitely. Shopify's only housekeeping is blunt: recent draft orders are deleted automatically after a year of inactivity, paper trail and all. The safer pattern is an expiry you control. Close the quote on a schedule you choose, archive what was agreed into the customer's record first, and send a courteous note offering a fresh quote at current prices.

How do I follow up on an unpaid invoice without annoying the customer?

Assume inertia, not refusal: most unpaid invoices are a buried email, not a changed mind. A nudge at day two that simply resurfaces the link clears a surprising share, because the customer already said yes and mostly needs the button put back in front of them. At a week, change the question from did you pay to does anything need changing, which gives silence a reason to speak; a meaningful share of stalled invoices turn out to be change requests. Match the register to the buyer: a first-time customer gets warmth, a regular gets brevity, and a business paying on terms gets the reminder sent to accounts payable before the due date, not after it. Cap the sequence at two or three touches, then end it cleanly by expiring the quote with a note that prices change and a fresh quote is one reply away. A deadline with a courteous exit reads as professionalism, not nagging, and it means silence has an ending instead of a permanent open loop.
